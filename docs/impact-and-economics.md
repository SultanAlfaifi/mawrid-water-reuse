# Impact and Economics

## 1. Measure use, not production

The amount entering the system is not the amount of drinking-quality water displaced. MAWRID reports a complete water balance:

```text
Collected greywater
= water delivered for reuse
+ reject or diversion flow
+ backwash and cleaning losses
+ overflow
+ change in stored volume
```

The principal impact metric is:

```text
Net water offset
= treated water actually delivered and used
− potable make-up
− treatment and backwash losses
```

All meters require calibration and a stated reconciliation error.

## 2. Site water balance

Before sizing a pilot, measure:

- Hourly and daily source-water availability.
- Hourly and seasonal demand for the selected end use.
- Required storage and maximum acceptable residence time.
- Periods of surplus, deficit, and no demand.
- Existing potable make-up and sewer-routing constraints.

Partial demand coverage can still create value. The system does not need to meet 100% of demand.

## 3. Economic framework

### Capital expenditure

- Source-separation plumbing and civil modifications.
- Raw and treated tanks.
- Pumps, valves, pipework, and containment.
- Screens, granular filters, and adsorption vessels.
- Disinfection equipment.
- Sensors, controller, electrical panel, and communications.
- Installation, commissioning, and initial laboratory validation.

### Operating expenditure

- Electricity.
- Disinfectant and other consumables.
- Biochar preparation, activation, transport, replacement, or regeneration.
- Screen cleaning, backwash, tank cleaning, and residual disposal.
- Sensor calibration and replacement.
- Laboratory testing.
- Labour, preventive maintenance, and corrective maintenance.
- Data service and reporting where applicable.

### Core economic metrics

```text
Annualized treatment cost (SAR/m³)
= annualized CAPEX + annual OPEX
  divided by treated water actually delivered and used
```

Also report annual water-cost avoidance, simple payback where appropriate, net present value under declared assumptions, and sensitivity to plumbing retrofit cost, media life, electricity, laboratory frequency, and water tariff.

## 4. Comparison cases

| Case | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Existing practice | Potable supply for the selected end use; greywater discharged |
| Sand + disinfection | Tests whether adsorption adds enough value to justify cost |
| Commercial activated carbon | Benchmarks local biochar performance and service life |
| Commercial onsite-reuse system | Benchmarks installed cost, certification, automation, and support |
| MAWRID candidate train | Evaluates the integrated local-media and condition-based approach |

Comparisons use equivalent capacity, end use, validation level, lifetime, and cost boundary.

## 5. Environmental accounting

Potential benefits include reduced demand for high-quality supply water, reduced immediate discharge, productive use of date-palm residues, and potentially lower treatment complexity than membrane-intensive alternatives.

These remain hypotheses until the boundary includes:

- Feedstock collection, transport, drying, and size reduction.
- Pyrolysis and activation energy and chemicals.
- Media washing and water use.
- Pumps, disinfection, controls, and communications.
- Backwash and cleaning.
- Media regeneration or disposal.
- Infrastructure lifetime and replacement.

MAWRID will not claim carbon neutrality, carbon negativity, or zero waste without a documented lifecycle assessment.

## 6. Illustrative calculation

> **ILLUSTRATIVE — not a MAWRID result**

If a site collects `5.0 m³/day`, delivers `4.0 m³/day` to a non-potable use, consumes `0.4 m³/day` in backwash and cleaning, and needs `0.2 m³/day` of potable make-up, then the dashboard must report each flow separately. It must not label all `5.0 m³/day` as water saved.

## 7. Commercial hypotheses

Potential models to test after technical validation include direct equipment sale with maintenance, a managed water-saving service, and replaceable media cartridges with monitoring. No recurring-revenue model is credible until media life, service burden, liability, compliance cost, and customer willingness to pay are measured.

New developments with planned greywater plumbing may offer better economics than retrofits. Centralized ablution areas in existing facilities may also be feasible when drainage separation is accessible.
